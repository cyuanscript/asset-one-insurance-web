Once you're a U.S. tax resident, geography stops mattering. It doesn't matter that the rental income comes from an apartment in Pudong, that the dividend is paid by a Hong Kong-listed company, or that the interest sits in a yuan-denominated deposit at a Chinese bank. If it's income, the IRS wants to know about it.

This surprises people because China taxes residents primarily on a more territorial-leaning basis with historically more limited worldwide enforcement, so the shift to true worldwide taxation is a real behavior change, not just a filing formality.

In practice, this means consolidating: rental income (calculated under U.S. rules, not Chinese ones), capital gains from selling Chinese property or securities, interest, dividends, and — for business owners — a share of foreign company profits that can trigger entirely separate anti-deferral regimes (GILTI, Subpart F) if you own 10% or more of a foreign corporation.

The saving grace is that the U.S. generally allows a Foreign Tax Credit for income taxes already paid to China on the same income, which prevents — though doesn't always fully eliminate — double taxation. Getting the credit calculation right, especially across different tax years and currencies, is where most self-filed returns go wrong.

The practical takeaway isn't to panic about disclosure — it's to get your foreign income streams organized and documented before your first U.S. tax year begins, so the first return isn't a scramble.
