For years, the assumption among some families was that a bank account in Hong Kong or a brokerage account in mainland China was simply invisible to U.S. authorities unless someone chose to disclose it. That assumption is now outdated, and has been for some time.

FATCA requires foreign financial institutions worldwide to identify U.S. account holders and report their account information directly to the IRS, or face a 30% withholding tax on certain U.S.-source payments passing through them — which is why Chinese and Hong Kong banks now routinely ask new account holders to declare U.S. tax status.

CRS, developed by the OECD, works in a similar spirit but among a much larger group of participating jurisdictions, exchanging financial account information between tax authorities globally — including information that can eventually reach Chinese authorities about accounts held abroad by Chinese tax residents, and vice versa for certain jurisdictions reporting to China.

Mainland China does not exchange information with the U.S. under CRS specifically (the U.S. operates through FATCA rather than CRS), but Hong Kong has been an active CRS participant for years, and many of the offshore structures Chinese families use — Hong Kong brokerage accounts, Singapore trusts, BVI entities — sit inside jurisdictions that do exchange under CRS.

The practical implication isn't that every account is automatically visible everywhere — it's that the assumption of invisibility is the wrong starting point for any planning conversation in 2026.
