FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and Form 8938 (the FATCA disclosure attached to your tax return) sound similar and often get confused, but they're filed with different agencies, under different rules, and sometimes both are required for the exact same accounts.

FBAR triggers if the combined highest balance across all your foreign financial accounts — checking, savings, brokerage, even certain foreign life insurance and pension-type policies with cash value — exceeds $10,000 at any point in the year. That threshold hasn't moved since the rule was created, and it's a lot easier to cross than people assume once you add up several accounts.

Form 8938 has higher, filing-status-dependent thresholds — for a married couple filing jointly and living in the U.S., reporting starts at $100,000 on the last day of the year or $150,000 at any point during it; for those living abroad, the thresholds roughly quadruple to $400,000 and $600,000.

> FBAR penalties are calculated per account, per year — which is how a modest oversight becomes a five-figure problem.

The penalties scale with willfulness. Non-willful failures can still run up to $10,000 per violation (adjusted for inflation); willful failures can reach the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance, per year. The IRS also has voluntary disclosure and streamlined filing programs for people who are behind and come forward before being contacted — a materially better outcome than being found first.

If you're not sure whether your accounts cross these lines, the answer is almost never "wait and see."
