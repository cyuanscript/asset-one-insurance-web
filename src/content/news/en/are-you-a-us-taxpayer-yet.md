Most people assume their U.S. tax status follows their visa status. It doesn't. The IRS uses two entirely separate tests, and either one alone is enough to make you a U.S. tax resident — with everything that implies for your worldwide income.

The first is simple: hold a green card at any point during the calendar year, and you're a resident for tax purposes from that day, full stop.

The second, the "substantial presence test," catches people who have no green card at all. Add up the days you were physically in the U.S. this year, one-third of the days last year, and one-sixth of the days the year before. If the total reaches 183, you're a resident — even on a business visa, a long stay with family, or repeated trips that quietly accumulate.

> Two years of frequent, unremarkable travel can add up to a tax status you never applied for.

There are exceptions — certain visa categories (students on F-1, teachers on J-1) are exempt from counting days for a period of time, and a "closer connection" exception can apply if you spent fewer than 183 days this year and can show your tax home and closer ties remain abroad. Both require proactive filing, not just being technically eligible.

Knowing which side of this line you're on — before your accountant tells you in April — is the first step in any cross-border tax conversation.
