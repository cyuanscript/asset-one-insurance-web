PFIC stands for Passive Foreign Investment Company, and the definition is broader than the name suggests. Nearly any non-U.S. pooled investment vehicle — a Chinese mutual fund, a Hong Kong unit trust, many ETFs domiciled outside the U.S. — qualifies if most of its income is passive (interest, dividends, capital gains) or most of its assets produce passive income. That covers the overwhelming majority of retail investment products sold in China and Hong Kong.

The default tax treatment, called the "excess distribution regime," is punishing by design. Gains and certain large distributions are spread evenly across your entire holding period, taxed at the highest ordinary income rate for each of those years, and hit with an interest charge on top — as if you owed the tax all along and simply hadn't paid it. There's no capital gains rate, no annual netting of losses, none of the usual mechanics investors expect.

> A fund that returned a modest, ordinary gain back home can generate a tax bill that looks like it belongs to a very different kind of investment.

There are two elections that soften this — a "qualified electing fund" (QEF) election, which requires cooperation from the fund itself to provide specific tax information most Chinese funds don't produce, and a "mark-to-market" election, which is more commonly usable but taxes unrealized annual gains as ordinary income. Making either election correctly, and on time, matters — a late election generally can't undo prior years' excess-distribution treatment.

The cleanest fix, in most cases, is simpler than untangling the PFIC math after the fact: restructure out of these holdings, into U.S.-domiciled funds or other vehicles, before you become a U.S. tax resident.
